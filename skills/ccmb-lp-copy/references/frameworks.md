# Landing Page Copy Frameworks

Five frameworks, when to use each, and the actual structure. Read only the section for the framework `/ccmb-lp-copy` picked. Don't blend frameworks — pick one, commit, draft.

---

## 1. PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solution

**Use for:** Low-ticket digital products ($7-97), pain-first audiences, cold traffic. Anyone who already knows their problem and is hunting for relief.

**Do not use for:** Identity-driven offers (use StoryBrand) or unaware audiences (use Schwartz).

### Structure

1. **Problem** (hero subhead or section 1) — name the exact problem in the reader's words. Not your interpretation of it. The reader should think: "This is for me."
2. **Agitate** (section 2) — describe what happens if it stays unsolved. Concrete consequences, not abstract pain. Time wasted, money lost, options narrowing, frustration compounding.
3. **Solution** (section 3 onward) — the product. Frame it as the *specific* fix for the *specific* problem. No category-wide claims.

### Hero patterns for PAS

- "Stop [losing X] every time you [try to Y]." → solution implied
- "Your [thing] keeps [breaking pattern]. Here's why — and the fix."
- "[Specific problem] — solved in [time / one product]."

### Agitate section — three good moves

- **Cost stack** — "Every week you don't [fix this], you lose [hours] / [dollars] / [opportunities]. Over a year that's [X]."
- **Compounding** — "It doesn't stay the same. It gets worse: [trajectory]."
- **The alternatives don't work** — "[Common alternative 1] solves a different problem. [Alternative 2] takes 3x as long. [Alternative 3] requires [thing the reader doesn't have]."

### Red flags

- Agitate that drifts into doom — readers tune out. Cap it at one section.
- "Are you tired of...?" openers — banned. Cliché shortcut.
- Solution section that lists features. PAS demands solution as *promise*, then mechanism.

## 2. AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**Use for:** SaaS, recurring products, anything with a free trial or freemium hook. AIDA works when the reader is browsing, not hunting.

### Structure

1. **Attention** — hero headline + visual. Stop the scroll. Bold outcome claim or surprising angle.
2. **Interest** — section 1-2. Why this matters to *you specifically.* Category positioning. "This is for [specific role] who [specific situation]."
3. **Desire** — sections 3-4. Proof + outcome. Testimonials, case studies, screenshots showing the after-state. Show what life is like with the product.
4. **Action** — clear single CTA. Free trial, demo, sign up, free tool.

### Hero patterns for AIDA

- "[Outcome] for [specific role]." → "Async deals for solo consultants."
- "The [category]-killer for [audience]." → "The Notion-killer for writers."
- "How [audience] [achieves outcome] without [common alternative]." → "How designers ship case studies without writing a single sentence."

### Interest section — the position move

Don't list features. State the category and your position in it. "Email tools are built for marketers. We built one for solo operators." This filters wrong-fit visitors out and locks right-fit visitors in.

### Desire section — the proof move

Three good moves:
- **Specific case study** — one user, named, with a real metric. "Sarah shipped 12 deals in 30 days with [product]." Beats 10 vague testimonials.
- **Visual after-state** — screenshot or short video of the product producing the outcome.
- **Quantified outcome** — "Average user saves 6.4 hours/week." Specificity > magnitude.

### Red flags

- Interest section that's a feature dump. Position, don't list.
- Desire built on logos with no context. "Used by Apple" means nothing without "the Apple [team] uses it for [specific job]."

## 3. StoryBrand — You Are the Hero, We Are the Guide

**Use for:** Identity-driven mid-ticket ($97-997). Courses, cohorts, community products, productized services. Any offer where the *transformation* matters more than the *deliverable*.

### Structure (the 7-part SB framework, compressed for LPs)

1. **A character** — the reader, named by identity. "Founders shipping their first paid AI product."
2. **Has a problem** — both external (the surface problem) and internal (the underlying fear or frustration).
3. **Meets a guide** — that's the brand. Brief credibility, not a hero shot.
4. **Who gives them a plan** — 3-step plan. Simple, named, sequential.
5. **And calls them to action** — clear CTA.
6. **That helps them avoid failure** — what they lose by not acting.
7. **And ends in success** — concrete after-state.

### Hero patterns for StoryBrand

- "Become the [identity] who [outcome]." → "Become the consultant who ships AI products on the side."
- "You're a [role]. You want [transformation]. Here's how." → "You're a writer. You want to ship a real product. Here's how, in 6 weeks."
- "For [identity] ready to [transformation]." → "For designers ready to stop trading hours for dollars."

### The 3-step plan section

Always 3 steps. Not 4, not 5. Each named with a verb. Each clear enough that a reader who skims gets the whole arc.

Example for a writing course:
1. **Set up** — install the skill, create your CLAUDE.md, pick your topic.
2. **Ship** — write and publish your first piece using the framework.
3. **Compound** — turn one piece into a system that ships weekly.

### Internal-problem section — the load-bearing move

External problem: "I want to ship an AI product."
Internal problem: "I feel like an impostor — I'm a writer, not a builder."

Naming the *internal* problem is what makes StoryBrand convert. The external problem is shared by every competitor's audience. The internal problem is what you uniquely understand. Lean on it.

### Red flags

- Brand-as-hero language. "We are the leading platform for..." → kill. Reader is hero. Brand is guide.
- 5-step plans. Reader sees 5 boxes and bounces. 3 always.
- Skipping the "avoid failure" section. Loss aversion converts.

## 4. Hormozi Grand Slam Offer

**Use for:** High-ticket services or programs ($1K+). Long-form scroll pages. Audiences willing to read 3000 words because they're evaluating a major purchase.

### Structure

1. **Hero** — outcome promise + "without X" framing. "Get [outcome] without [common cost]."
2. **Dream outcome** — paint the after-state in concrete sensory detail.
3. **Perceived likelihood of achievement** — proof, case studies, specific metrics. The reader thinks "this could work for me."
4. **Time delay** — how fast results show up. "Within 4 weeks" / "By day 30" — never "eventually."
5. **Effort and sacrifice** — minimized but honest. "We do X for you. You handle Y." Not "totally hands-off" (lie). Not "you do everything" (no value).
6. **Value stack** — itemized list of every deliverable, each with a named dollar value. Stack to a number that dwarfs the price.
7. **Price reveal** — `Total value: $X,XXX. Your price: $X.` The gap is the offer.
8. **Risk reversal** — money-back guarantee, results guarantee, or stronger ("if you don't ship in 4 weeks, you get next cohort free + I'll work with you 1:1").
9. **Urgency** — real, not fake. Cohort cap of 30 seats. Pricing rises Friday. Doors close Sunday.
10. **CTA** — single, clear, repeated 3-4 times throughout the scroll.

### Hero patterns for Hormozi

- "How [audience] [outcome] in [time] — without [common pain]." → "How consultants 2x their rate in 90 days without cold outreach."
- "The [outcome] system for [audience] who refuse to [common alternative]."
- "From [before-state] to [after-state] in [time] — guaranteed."

### Value stack — the load-bearing move

The whole framework is the stack. If you have:
- The core offer ($2000 value)
- A bonus 1:1 onboarding call ($500 value)
- A second bonus group call ($1000 value)
- A third bonus — template library or community access ($500 value)
- A fourth bonus — direct Slack access ($1000 value)

Total value: $5000. Your price: $997. The math is the persuasion.

Caveat: **the values must be defensible.** If "Slack access" is valued at $1000 but nobody would pay $1000 for Slack access, the reader smells it and the page collapses. Stack honestly or use a different framework.

### Risk reversal — three tiers

- **Conditional refund:** "If you don't [outcome] in 4 weeks, full refund."
- **Asymmetric:** "If you don't [outcome], refund + you keep all materials."
- **Better-than-money-back:** "If you don't [outcome], I'll work with you 1:1 until you do."

Stronger reversal → higher conversion at the same price.

### Red flags

- Fake urgency. "Only 3 spots left!" that's been there for 6 months. Burn trust permanently.
- Inflated stack values. Reader's BS detector is tuned.
- No risk reversal. At $1K+ you must have one. Without it, conversion craters.

## 5. Schwartz 5 Levels of Awareness

**Use for:** Gating the headline. This isn't a structure for the whole page — it's a *headline-level decision* that runs through every other framework.

### The 5 levels

| Level | Awareness | Headline approach |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unaware — doesn't know they have the problem | Pitch the problem. Tell a story that makes them feel it. "You've been [doing thing]. Here's what it's costing you." |
| 2 | Problem-aware — knows the problem, doesn't know solutions exist | Pitch the *category* of solution. "There's a way to [outcome]. It's called [category]." |
| 3 | Solution-aware — knows solutions exist, hasn't picked one | Pitch *your category vs alternatives.* "Most [category] tools do X. We do Y." |
| 4 | Product-aware — knows your product, hasn't bought | Pitch *the offer* — pricing, bonuses, guarantees, urgency. |
| 5 | Most aware — ready to buy, just needs the trigger | Pitch the deal. "$497 → $297 until Friday." |

### How to use this with the other frameworks

- PAS headlines default to **level 2** ("Stop losing X every time you Y") — problem-aware.
- StoryBrand headlines default to **level 3** ("For founders ready to ship") — solution-aware, identity-positioned.
- AIDA headlines default to **level 3** ("The Notion-killer for writers") — solution-aware, category-positioned.
- Hormozi headlines default to **level 4** ("How consultants 2x their rate") — product-aware, outcome-led.

**The mismatch trap:** Writing a level-3 headline for a level-1 audience. The headline assumes the reader knows things they don't. They bounce. Test: does your audience already know that solutions to this problem exist? If no, you're at level 1 or 2 — adjust headline down.

### Cold traffic = lower level

Paid ads = mostly level 1-2. SEO long-tail = mostly level 2-3. Direct/referral traffic = level 3-4. Pricing-page traffic = level 4-5.

If you don't know your traffic source, default to level 2 (problem-aware). Safest middle ground.

## 6. Picking — when in doubt

| Situation | Pick |
|---|---|
| Free thing, email capture | Single-promise hero, no framework |
| First time launching anything | PAS — fewest moving parts |
| You can't articulate what makes you different | StoryBrand — identity replaces differentiation |
| You have lots of social proof | AIDA — proof carries the page |
| You have $1K+ price and stack of bonuses | Hormozi |
| You have $1K+ price and no stack | Drop the price or build the stack — Hormozi without stack collapses |
| Cold paid traffic | PAS at Schwartz level 2 |
| Warm email list | AIDA at Schwartz level 3-4 |
